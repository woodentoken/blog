Aircraft are incredibly rich systems. Their dynamics change constantly with their environment. The introduction of aircraft 120 years ago has revolutionized travel, reconaissance, and warfare. Increasingly, small aircraft have become a part of civil applications, including recreation, agriculture, medical access, mapping, and the delivery of goods. Yet the aircraft that humans have developed are fundamentally limited. Our aircraft are rigid, and their control relatively simplistic. This rigidity means that they adapt only minimally to their environment, and their control actuation is more a result of manufacturing simplicity than a thorough exploration of their dynamics. Furthermore, our fixed wing aircraft generally fly in a very small band of aerodynamic effects, -10 to 15 degrees angle of attack.

When we survey the broad and impressive usage of fixed wing aircraft to date, we see them as capable and highly engineered systems. But aircraft lack the robustness, agility, maneuverability, and broad applicability of their natural forebears, organisms like birds, bats, and insects.

These natural fliers fly in extremely complex environments, where gusts form a substantial part of their forward speed - yet they adapt their shape, control, and dynamics in real time to maintain stability and control. Birds are capable of hovering, gliding, and maneuvering in ways that are not possible for rigid aircraft. This adaptation comes from their flexible bodies and their overactuated control, affording them many more options for navigating the world. In addition, they fly very efficiently.

Yet nature does not yield optimums, and birds, bats, and insects need to eat, mate, and defend themselves. These impose naturalistic constraints on their flight, many of which are unnecessary for human application. Therefore, there exists a rich problem space where we can take information from biological flight and apply the *salient* features to engineered aircraft.

My research seeks to understand this information process in the domain of flight dynamics and control.
