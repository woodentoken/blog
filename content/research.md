Aircraft are  rich systems. Their dynamics are in a constant state of change.

The discovery of aircraft 120 years ago has revolutionized travel, reconnaissance, economics, and warfare. Increasingly, small aircraft have found civil applications, including agriculture, medical accessibility, mapping, inspection, and the delivery of goods.

Yet the aircraft we engineer are fundamentally limited. They are designed to be rigid, and their control is relatively simplistic. Rigidity means that they adapt only minimally to their environment, sometimes unintentionally.Their control actuation is more a result of manufacturing simplicity and weight feasibility than a thorough exploration of their dynamics. Furthermore, our fixed wing aircraft generally fly in a very small band of the aerodynamic atlas; -10 to 15 degrees angle of attack, low sideslip.

While these are effective, aircraft lack the robustness, agility, maneuverability, and broad applicability of their natural forebears, organisms like birds, bats, and insects.

These organisms fly in extremely complex environments, where gusts and atmospheric turbulence form a substantial part of their forward speed - yet they adapt their shape, control, and dynamics in real time to maintain their flight path. Birds are capable of hovering, gliding, and maneuvering in ways that are not possible for rigid aircraft, despite being a similar size and weight. This adaptation comes from their flexible bodies and their overactuated controls, affording them many more options for navigating the world. They also manage to fly extremely efficiently, extracting energy from the wind they move through.

But evolution is not optimization; birds, bats, and insects need to eat, mate, and defend themselves. These impose naturalistic constraints on their flight, many of which are unnecessary for human application. Therein exists a rich problem space where we can take information from biological flight and apply the *salient* features to engineered aircraft.

My research seeks to understand this information process in the domain of flight dynamics and control. What does morphing permit in terms of flight maneuverability and adaptability? How can we understand the implications of morphing for more capable aircraft.
